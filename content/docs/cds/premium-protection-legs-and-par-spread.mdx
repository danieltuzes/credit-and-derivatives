## What a CDS is

A credit default swap (CDS) is a bilateral derivative on a named reference
entity. The protection buyer pays a running premium to the protection seller.
If a covered credit event occurs before maturity, the seller makes a
loss-related protection payment. Unlike buying the entity's bond, entering a
CDS does not itself lend principal to the reference entity. Tuckman and Serrat
describe the fee leg and the contingent leg (in this course, the premium leg
and the protection leg) in Chapter 14[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; §14.5, printed pp. 361–362]; Hull describes the same cash-flow directions for buyer and
seller, and the quarterly premium convention[@hull-options-futures; §24.1, printed pp. 548–550].

This lesson reports both leg present values as positive magnitudes. From the
protection buyer's perspective, the net present value is the protection-leg
present value minus the premium-leg present value:

$$
\explain{cds-protection-buyer-net-present-value}{PV_0^{\mathrm{buyer}}}
=\explain{cds-protection-leg-present-value}{PV_0^{\mathrm{prot}}}
-\explain{cds-premium-leg-present-value}{PV_0^{\mathrm{prem}}}.
$$

The seller has the opposite signed value in this two-party model.

## Start from disjoint events

Let the random [[cds-default-time]] be $\tau$. Use the local
[[cds-period-index]] $i$ across the
[[number-of-cds-periods]] $n$. The schedule has dates
$0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=T$. Define the disjoint interval events

$$
\explain{cds-default-event}{G_i}
=\{\explain{payment-time}{t_{i-1}}<
\explain{cds-default-time}{\tau}\leq
\explain{payment-time}{t_i}\},
\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,
$$

and add $\explain{cds-default-event}{G_0}=\{\tau>T\}$ for survival through
maturity. These events are
disjoint and exhaustive under the one-default assumption.

For a local [[cds-event-payoff]] $Y$ that is constant at $y_i$ on each
event, the risk-neutral expectation is:

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\explain{cds-event-payoff}{Y}]
=\sum_{i=0}^{n}
\explain{cds-event-payoff-value}{y_i}\,
\explain{risk-neutral-probability-measure}{\mathbb{Q}}
(\explain{cds-default-event}{G_i}).
$$

This is the [[expectation]] from disjoint events of the probability lesson,
with risk-neutral pricing weights. If $Y$ is not constant within an interval,
the expectation uses the conditional means:

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\explain{cds-event-payoff}{Y}]
=\sum_{i=0}^{n}
\explain{risk-neutral-probability-measure}{\mathbb{Q}}
(\explain{cds-default-event}{G_i})
\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\explain{cds-event-payoff}{Y}\mid\explain{cds-default-event}{G_i}].
$$

For this reason, one payment at the end of the period is not an exact treatment
of a default in the middle of the period. If every interval is divided into
smaller default-time intervals, the limit of the sum is an integral. Shreve gives the finite
disjoint-event expectation rule used here[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 1 §1.3, printed pp. 13–18]. Tuckman and Serrat's CDS table also weights
cash flows by survival and default events[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; §14.6, printed pp. 368–370, Table 14.10 and eqs. 14.6–14.7].

## The hazard rate gives the probabilities in the formulas

Let $\explain{cds-general-hazard-rate}{\lambda(u)}$ be the risk-neutral
default intensity at time $\explain{cds-default-integration-time}{u}$, given
survival to $u$. It is a deterministic function of time, measured per
model-year under the [[risk-neutral-probability-measure]] $\mathbb{Q}$. It
generalizes the constant [[hazard-rate]] $\lambda$ of this lesson. Its
meaning is the conditional intensity of the credit lesson, but it need not be
constant: $\lambda(u)$ is the proportional rate at which the survival
probability decreases at $u$, given survival to $u$. As a differential
equation, this meaning is:

$$
-\,d\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,u)}
=\explain{cds-general-hazard-rate}{\lambda(u)}\,
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,u)}\,d
\explain{cds-default-integration-time}{u},
\qquad
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,0)}=1.
$$

Dividing by the (positive) survival probability turns the left side into a
derivative of its logarithm, $-d\ln
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,u)}=
\explain{cds-general-hazard-rate}{\lambda(u)}\,d
\explain{cds-default-integration-time}{u}$. Integrating from $0$ to
$\explain{payment-time}{t}$ and using $\ln
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,0)}=\ln1=0$ gives the survival
probability:

$$
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t)}
=\exp\left(-\int_0^{\explain{payment-time}{t}}
\explain{cds-general-hazard-rate}{\lambda(u)}\,d
\explain{cds-default-integration-time}{u}\right).
$$

Integrating the same differential equation over one period, not from $0$,
gives the interval default probability for a general hazard rate:

$$
\explain{interval-default-probability}{\Delta q_i^{\mathbb{Q}}}
=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}
\explain{cds-general-hazard-rate}{\lambda(u)}\,
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,u)}\,d
\explain{cds-default-integration-time}{u}
=\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t_{i-1})}
-\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t_i)}.
$$

The constant [[hazard-rate]] $\lambda$ of this lesson is the special case
$\lambda(u)\equiv\lambda$. Then $\int_0^t\lambda(u)\,du=\lambda t$, and the
two results above become:

$$
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t)}=\exp(-\lambda t),
\qquad
\explain{interval-default-probability}{\Delta q_i^{\mathbb{Q}}}
=\exp(-\lambda t_{i-1})-\exp(-\lambda t_i).
$$

The next two sections, on the premium leg and on the protection leg, use the
general $\lambda(u)$. The section on closed forms after them uses the constant
$\lambda$.

These probabilities are pricing probabilities, not forecasts, for a constant
and for a general hazard rate. A hazard rate implied from CDS prices can be a
risk-neutral quantity rather than a prediction of realized defaults[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; §14.7, printed p. 371]. The constant-hazard survival relation is given in §14.6 and
Appendix A14.1[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; printed pp. 367–368 and 505, eqs. 14.4–14.5 and A14.1–A14.2].

## Default in the middle of a period

Suppose default occurs at $u$ in $(t_{i-1},t_i]$. The input
[[recovery-rate]] is $R$.

- The scheduled premium at $t_i$ is not paid, because the reference entity did
  not survive to that date.
- The buyer pays premium accrued from $t_{i-1}$ to $u$. In this model its year
  fraction is
  $\explain{cds-accrued-year-fraction-at-default}{a_i(u)}=u-t_{i-1}$.
- The seller pays protection of
  $\explain{loss-given-default}{\mathrm{LGD}}N=(1-R)N$.
- Both amounts triggered by default are discounted from the modeled default
  time $u$, not from $t_i$.

Hull notes that a final accrued premium is paid when default occurs
between scheduled dates[@hull-options-futures; §24.1, printed pp. 548–550].
The half-period and midpoint calculations in Hull and in Tuckman and Serrat
are discrete approximations[@hull-options-futures; §24.2, printed pp. 551–554, Tables 24.1–24.4][@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; §14.6, printed pp. 368–370, Table 14.10]. This lesson integrates over the modeled default time exactly, for a
general hazard rate and a general discount curve. The closed forms later in
the lesson use a constant hazard rate and a constant interest rate.

## Exact premium-leg expectation in this model

Let $\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,u)}$ be the general dated discount curve of
the rates lesson; this section does not assume a flat rate. The
[[cds-scheduled-premium-annuity]] is:

$$
\explain{cds-scheduled-premium-annuity}{A_0^{\mathrm{sched}}}
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\explain{cds-accrual-year-fraction}{\alpha_i}
\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,t_i)}
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t_i)}.
$$

The exact [[cds-accrued-premium-annuity]] is:

$$
\explain{cds-accrued-premium-annuity}{A_0^{\mathrm{accr}}}
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}
\explain{cds-accrued-year-fraction-at-default}{a_i(u)}
\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,u)}
\explain{cds-general-hazard-rate}{\lambda(u)}
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,u)}\,\mathrm{d}u.
$$

The [[cds-premium-annuity]] is the sum of the two annuities. The premium-leg
present value is the annual spread, as a decimal, multiplied by the notional
and by the premium annuity:

$$
\explain{cds-premium-annuity}{A_0^{\mathrm{prem}}}
=\explain{cds-scheduled-premium-annuity}{A_0^{\mathrm{sched}}}
+\explain{cds-accrued-premium-annuity}{A_0^{\mathrm{accr}}},
\qquad
\explain{cds-premium-leg-present-value}{PV_0^{\mathrm{prem}}}
=\explain{cds-contract-spread}{s}
\explain{cds-notional}{N}
\explain{cds-premium-annuity}{A_0^{\mathrm{prem}}}.
\label{eq:exact-cds-premium-leg}
$$

A spread quoted in [[basis-point]] per year is divided by 10,000 before it
is used in [[eq-exact-cds-premium-leg]].

## Exact protection-leg expectation in this model

The protection-leg present value, as a positive magnitude, is:

$$
\explain{cds-protection-leg-present-value}{PV_0^{\mathrm{prot}}}
=\explain{cds-notional}{N}
\explain{loss-given-default}{\mathrm{LGD}}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}
\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,u)}
\explain{cds-general-hazard-rate}{\lambda(u)}
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,u)}\,\mathrm{d}u.
\label{eq:exact-cds-protection-leg}
$$

Equation [[eq-exact-cds-protection-leg]] is an expectation. Each possible
payoff at a default time is discounted and multiplied by the risk-neutral
probability density of that default time. The integral then adds over all
default times.

## Closed forms and the zero-upfront par spread

This section uses a constant [[hazard-rate]], $\lambda(u)\equiv\lambda$,
which is a special case of the [[cds-general-hazard-rate]]. It also uses a
constant [[continuously-compounded-risk-free-rate]] $r$. So the general
discount curve of the last two sections is
$\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,u)}=\exp(-ru)$.

For the regular model-year schedule, $\alpha_i=t_i-t_{i-1}$ and the
[[maturity-time]] is $T=t_n$. Define the abbreviation
$\explain{cds-combined-decay-rate}{\kappa}=r+\lambda$. Then the scheduled-premium
annuity and the accrued-premium annuity are:

$$
\explain{cds-scheduled-premium-annuity}{A_0^{\mathrm{sched}}}
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\exp(-\kappa t_i),
$$

$$
\explain{cds-accrued-premium-annuity}{A_0^{\mathrm{accr}}}
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\lambda\exp(-\kappa t_{i-1})
\frac{1-\exp(-\kappa\alpha_i)(1+\kappa\alpha_i)}{\kappa^2},
$$

The [[cds-protection-factor]], per unit of notional, is:

$$
\explain{cds-protection-factor}{B_0^{\mathrm{prot}}}
=\mathrm{LGD}\,\lambda
\frac{1-\exp(-\kappa T)}{\kappa}.
$$

At $\kappa=0$, the continuous limits replace the two fractions by
$\alpha_i^2/2$ and $T$, respectively. The implementation uses numerically stable
forms of those limits.

The [[cds-par-spread]] $s^\star$ is the running spread that makes the legs
equal with no upfront amount:

$$
\explain{cds-par-spread}{s^\star}
=\frac{\explain{cds-protection-factor}{B_0^{\mathrm{prot}}}}
{\explain{cds-premium-annuity}{A_0^{\mathrm{prem}}}},
\qquad
PV_0^{\mathrm{prem}}(s^\star)=PV_0^{\mathrm{prot}}.
$$

Tuckman and Serrat state the condition that the fair spread makes the two legs
equal, and the relationship between spread and upfront[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; §14.6, printed pp. 370–371, and Appendix A14.2, printed pp. 506–507]. The next lesson distinguishes
this model par spread from a standard running coupon and a signed upfront
amount.

## Generalizing the premium leg: an extinguishing swap

This lesson uses the name **extinguishing swap** for the construction below.
The name belongs to this course; it is not a standard market term or textbook
term. An extinguishing swap is any schedule of cash flows that stops
permanently at the default of the reference entity. The premium leg above has
this property.

Consider the same dates $0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=T$ and the same reference
entity. Replace the scheduled premium
$\explain{cds-contract-spread}{s}\explain{cds-notional}{N}
\explain{cds-accrual-year-fraction}{\alpha_i}$ at each date with an arbitrary
deterministic, signed net cash flow
$\explain{cds-extinguishing-swap-cash-flow}{c_i}$ for one named perspective.
Under the signed cash-flow convention of this course, a positive $c_i$ is
received and a negative $c_i$ is paid. As with the premium leg, a scheduled
amount is paid only on survival to its date. Every later scheduled amount is
extinguished at the default time. In this lesson, $c_i$ is an input and
deterministic; it does not depend on a floating rate. If a cash flow accrues
continuously between dates, an accrued-at-default term can be added with the
same construction as the [[cds-accrued-premium-annuity]]. This lesson does not
repeat that term.

The expectation from disjoint events, used for both CDS legs above, gives the
[[cds-extinguishing-swap-present-value]] of the scheduled cash flows. It is the
same survival-weighted discounting that gave $A_0^{\mathrm{sched}}$:

$$
\explain{cds-extinguishing-swap-present-value}{PV_0^{\mathrm{ext}}}
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\explain{cds-extinguishing-swap-cash-flow}{c_i}\,
\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,t_i)}\,
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t_i)}.
$$

With $\explain{cds-extinguishing-swap-cash-flow}{c_i}=
\explain{cds-contract-spread}{s}\explain{cds-notional}{N}
\explain{cds-accrual-year-fraction}{\alpha_i}$ for every $i$, the present value
is $sNA_0^{\mathrm{sched}}$, the scheduled-payment term of the premium leg. So
the premium leg is the special case of an extinguishing swap whose cash flows
are one running spread paid on a fixed notional.

$$

Both terms use the same $D(0,t_i)S(0,t_i)$ factors as the premium leg. Only
the signed cash flows $c_i$ are different from the premium leg's
$sN\alpha_i$.

## Why there is no volatility input here

Risk-neutral valuation does not remove volatility. A change from the
real-world measure to $\mathbb{Q}$ changes the pricing probabilities, and in
common diffusion models it changes the drift. It does not change the size of
the random fluctuations. Shreve shows that the Brownian volatility term remains after the risk-neutral change of
measure[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 5 §5.2.2, printed pp. 214–217].

This CDS model needs no separate volatility parameter for two reasons. First,
its risk-free rate, hazard rate, and recovery rate are deterministic. Second,
both legs are expectations that are linear in the cash flows. In other cases,
the distributions, the dependence, and the volatility of the inputs could
matter: if rates, the hazard rate, or recovery were stochastic, or if the
product were a CDS option, had wrong-way risk, or had nonlinear exposure. The
risk-neutral measure gives the pricing weights. The restrictive model
assumptions, not the measure, remove the need for a separate volatility input.

## Model boundary and review note

The valuation is exact only inside this simplified model. The premium leg and
the protection leg are derived for a general, possibly time-varying
risk-neutral hazard rate and a general dated discount curve. The closed forms,
the numeric examples, the explorer, and the assessment use a special case: one
constant risk-neutral hazard rate, one deterministic continuously compounded
risk-free rate, a deterministic recovery rate, and settlement at the default
time. The extinguishing swap is a construction of this lesson that generalizes
the premium leg; it is not a named market instrument. The model omits calendar
schedules,
day counts, business-day rules, payment delays, term-structure calibration,
auctions, deliverables, credit-event legal definitions, counterparty risk,
collateral, funding, restructuring, and transaction costs. All materially
changed content remains \`draft\` pending independent human review.
