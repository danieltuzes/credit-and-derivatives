## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- explain why the price of a bond with fixed positive cash flows decreases when
  the yield increases;
- hold contract cash flows fixed while changing only the discounting input;
- recognize that the price-yield relationship is curved rather than linear.

## The price decreases because every denominator increases

Write the [[bond-payment-frequency]] as $m_{\mathrm B}$. In this lesson,
$y$ denotes the [[yield-to-maturity]] $y^{(m_{\mathrm B})}$ without its
superscript, as the argument of a function. Write the
[[price-yield-curve]] as $P(y)$.

Use the [[bond-payment-index]] $k$ to select one of the
[[number-of-bond-payments]] $n$, and write each promised positive
[[bond-cash-flow]] as $CF_k^{\mathrm{bond}}$. The price at yield $y$ is the
sum of the promised cash flows, each discounted at the yield:

$$
P(y)=
\sum_{k=1}^{n}
\frac{CF_k^{\mathrm{bond}}}
{\left(1+y/m_{\mathrm B}\right)^k}.
$$

Assume that the yield stays non-negative and that every cash flow stays fixed.
If the yield increases, every denominator increases. So every discounted cash
flow decreases, and the [[bond-price]] decreases. The table below also assumes
that the new yield is non-negative.

Define the local [[yield-shock]] $\Delta y$ as new yield minus old
yield, and define the local [[price-change]] $\Delta P$ as new price
minus old price.

| Yield shock  | Denominators | Price change |
| ------------ | ------------ | ------------ |
| $\Delta y>0$ | Increase     | $\Delta P<0$ |
| $\Delta y<0$ | Decrease     | $\Delta P>0$ |

The table holds only if the promised cash flows stay fixed.

  <CompactExample label="Three points form a curve">

Consider a five-year bond with an annual coupon rate of 5%, semiannual
payments, and a face value of 100. Its prices at three yields are:

| Nominal annual yield | Price per 100 face |
| -------------------: | -----------------: |
|                   5% |           100.0000 |
|                   6% |            95.7349 |
|                   7% |            91.6834 |

The price decreases as the yield increases. Each yield increase is one
percentage point, but the two price changes are different: 4.2651 and 4.0515.
So the three points are not on a straight line.

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Equal shocks, unequal price changes">

Start from a yield of 5%:

- a yield decrease to 4% increases the price from 100.0000 to 104.4913, a
  change of +4.4913;
- a yield increase to 6% decreases the price from 100.0000 to 95.7349, a
  change of −4.2651.

The two yield shocks have equal size, but the absolute price changes are
different. This difference is a result of the curvature of the price-yield
curve. This lesson does not calculate duration, DV01, or convexity.

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Hold other inputs fixed">

Suppose the issuer changes a coupon, a default occurs, or the settlement date
changes. Then more than the yield has changed, and the comparison no longer
isolates the effect of the yield.

  </CompactExample>

## Model boundary and review note

The plotted curve summarizes a toy model with one yield. It is not a credit
curve and not a recommendation.
It omits accrued interest, term-structure effects, default, recovery, liquidity,
tax, funding, and embedded options.

The inverse direction and the curvature both follow Tuckman &
Serrat[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; §4.1, "Price-Rate Curves", Figure 4.2, prices decrease as rates rise; §4.5, "Convexity", price-rate curves of coupon bonds are convex]; Hull notes the same negative price-yield
relationship[@hull-options-futures; §4.8, Duration]. FINRA states that price and yield are inversely
related[@finra-bond-yield; "Price and yield" section]. The lesson stays
`draft` pending human confirmation of the printed locators.
