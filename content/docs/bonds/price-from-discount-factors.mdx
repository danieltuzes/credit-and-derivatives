## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to calculate a toy bond's price from its
promised cash-flow schedule and input discount factors.

## Bond pricing is a present-value application

Use the [[bond-payment-index]] $k$ to select one of the
[[number-of-bond-payments]] $n$. Write the promised
[[bond-cash-flow]] as $CF_k^{\mathrm{bond}}$, its
[[payment-time]] as $t_k$, and its matching [[discount-factor]] as
$D(0,t_k)$. Write the [[bond-price]] as $P_0$. The bond price is the [[present-value]]
at [[valuation-time]] $0$ of the promised payments. So the bond price is the
schedule sum [[foundations/present-value#eq-present-value-sum]], applied to the
promised payments of a bond:

$$
P_0=\sum_{k=1}^{n}CF_k^{\mathrm{bond}}D(0,t_k).
\label{eq:bond-price-from-factors}
$$

In this lesson, “price” means this present value. It does not mean a clean
price, a quoted price, or a probability. Those concepts are outside this
lesson.

  <CompactExample label="Two discount factors">

The annual-coupon bond from the preceding lesson pays USD 50 after one year and
USD 1,050 after two years. Suppose the matching discount factors are 0.96 and 0.91.

| Payment index $k$ | Cash flow, USD | Discount factor | Present value, USD |
| ----------------: | -------------: | --------------: | -----------------: |
|                 1 |             50 |            0.96 |              48.00 |
|                 2 |          1,050 |            0.91 |             955.50 |

The bond price is the sum of the present values:

$$
P_0=50(0.96)+1050(0.91)=\text{USD }1{,}003.50.
$$

The price is above USD 1,000 because, with these two discount factors, the
present value of the promised payments is larger than the face value. This
lesson does not use a yield.

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Unfamiliar schedule">

Consider payments of USD 20 at 0.5 years, USD 20 at 1.0 year, and USD 1,020 at
1.5 years, with discount factors 0.98, 0.95, and 0.92. The bond price is:

$$
P_0
=20(0.98)+20(0.95)+1020(0.92)
=19.60+19.00+938.40
=\text{USD }977.00.
$$

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Zero-coupon boundary">

If every coupon is zero, the only payment is the [[face-value]] $F$ at
[[maturity-time]] $T$. The bond price is then:

$$
P_0=F D(0,T).
$$

This is [[eq-bond-price-from-factors]] with one payment. It is not a separate
rule.

  </CompactExample>
</CompactExamples>

## Error pattern: discount after adding

For the two-payment example, compare a wrong calculation, which applies one
discount factor to the sum of the payments, with the calculation in
[[eq-bond-price-from-factors]]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(50+1050)(0.91) &= 1001.00,\\
50(0.96)+1050(0.91) &= 1003.50,\\
1001.00 &\ne 1003.50.
\end{aligned}
$$

One common discount factor is correct only when the model assigns that
discount factor to every payment.

## Model boundary and review note

The discount factors are inputs; they are not calibrated. This lesson has no
credit risk, although the course is about credit products.

The pricing rule [[eq-bond-price-from-factors]] follows Tuckman &
Serrat[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; §1.2, each bond price as its cash flows times the matching discount factors (eqs. 1.1-1.3), checked against market prices in Table 1.4]; Hull discounts each cash flow at its own zero rate the
same way[@hull-options-futures; Ch. 4, "Bond Pricing"]. The lesson
stays `draft` pending human confirmation of the printed locators.
