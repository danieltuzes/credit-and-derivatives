## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- interpret recovery as a fraction of a named base paid at a named time;
- calculate loss given default as the complement of recovery;
- calculate present value in one explicitly labeled
  recovery-of-par-paid-at-maturity model.

## Name the recovery convention before calculating

This lesson uses one recovery convention. A claim has a positive local
[[recovery-par-amount]] $F$ and one local
[[recovery-payment-time]] $T$, measured from
[[valuation-time]] $0$.

- If the reference entity survives through $T$, the holder receives $F$ at
  $T$.
- If the reference entity defaults at or before $T$, the holder receives $RF$
  at $T$, where the [[recovery-rate]] $R$ is deterministic.

The survival payment and the default payment are both paid at $T$. This timing
is a model assumption, not a description of an actual instrument. This lesson
does not teach recovery paid at default, recovery of market value, settlement
delay, or a CDS settlement mechanism.

Tuckman and Serrat define a bond recovery rate relative to the face amount.
They also identify the complementary loss fraction in their credit-loss
discussion [@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; Ch. 14 §14.2, printed p. 353].
Payment of the recovery at maturity is a convention of this lesson. The lesson
does not claim that it is the only recovery convention.

## Recovery and loss given default are complements here

The [[loss-given-default]] fraction is defined as:

$$
\mathrm{LGD}=1-R,
\qquad 0\leq R\leq1.
$$

For example, $R=0.40$ gives $\mathrm{LGD}=0.60$ in this toy model. The value
0.40 is chosen only for the arithmetic. The lesson makes no claim that 40% is a
market convention, a forecast, or an appropriate assumption for any real
entity.

## Calculate the expected maturity amount, then discount it

The input [[survival-probability]] is
$\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,T)}$ under the
[[risk-neutral-probability-measure]] $\mathbb{Q}$. In the one-default model,
the probability of default by $T$ is $1-S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,T)$.

For the single interval from $0$ through $T$, $1-S(0,T)$ is the
[[interval-default-probability]]. The expected amount paid at $T$ under
$\mathbb{Q}$ is the survival payment and the default payment, each multiplied by
its probability:

$$
F\left[S(0,T)+R\left(1-S(0,T)\right)\right].
$$

This is the two-event case of the expectation from disjoint events in the
probability lesson: survival pays $F$, and default pays $RF$. Shreve gives the
expectation of a finite-valued random variable[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 1 §1.3, printed pp. 13–18].
Tuckman and Serrat weight credit cash flows by survival and default
probabilities and then discount them[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; Ch. 14 §14.7, printed pp. 371–372, eq. 14.8 and Table 14.11].

The survival payment and the default payment are both paid at $T$. So one input
[[discount-factor]] $\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,T)}$ applies to both. The
[[recovery-of-par-present-value]] is the discounted expected amount:

$$
\explain{recovery-of-par-present-value}{PV_0^{\mathrm{RoP}}}
=F\,\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,T)}
\left[
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,T)}
+R\left(1-\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,T)}\right)
\right].
$$

This [[present-value]] is calculated from the perspective of the holder of the
claim: the modeled future receipts and their value are positive. A purchase
price paid by the holder would have the opposite cash-flow sign.

<CompactExamples
  summary="Worked examples"
  hint="Open the set, then choose a recovery boundary."
  tabsLabel="Recovery-of-par present-value examples"
>
  <CompactExample label="Partial recovery">

Let $F=\text{USD }1{,}000$, $D(0,T)=0.96$, $S(0,T)=0.92$, and $R=0.40$. The
expected fraction of par paid at maturity is:

$$
0.92+0.40(1-0.92)=0.952.
$$

Multiplying this fraction by the par amount and the discount factor gives the
present value:

$$
PV_0^{\mathrm{RoP}}
=1000(0.96)(0.952)
=\text{USD }913.92.
$$

The expected amount at maturity, before discounting, is USD 952.00. The
discount factor converts this amount to its value at valuation time.

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Zero-recovery boundary">

Let $F=\text{USD }1{,}000$, $D(0,T)=0.94$, $S(0,T)=0.90$, and $R=0$. The
default payment is zero, so the present value is:

$$
PV_0^{\mathrm{RoP}}
=1000(0.94)\left[0.90+0(0.10)\right]
=\text{USD }846.00.
$$

With zero recovery, the present value is the discounted expected survival
payment. This example makes no statement about real recovery outcomes.

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Full-recovery boundary">

Keep $F=\text{USD }1{,}000$, $D(0,T)=0.94$, and $S(0,T)=0.90$, but set $R=1$.
The survival payment and the default payment are now both the full par amount,
paid at $T$:

$$
PV_0^{\mathrm{RoP}}
=1000(0.94)\left[0.90+1(0.10)\right]
=\text{USD }940.00.
$$

With full recovery, the present value does not depend on the survival
probability. The reason is that both modeled events pay the same amount on the
same date. This result holds only under the timing convention of this lesson.

  </CompactExample>
</CompactExamples>

## Model boundary and review note

The model has one maturity date, one deterministic recovery rate, one input
survival probability, and one input discount factor. Recovery is a fraction of
par paid at maturity, also when default occurs earlier. The lesson omits:

- settlement at the default time;
- coupons and multiple intervals;
- stochastic recovery and recovery of market value;
- dependence between interest rates and default;
- calibration;
- liquidity, funding, taxes, and counterparty risk.

All materially changed content and source locators remain \`draft\` pending
independent human review.
