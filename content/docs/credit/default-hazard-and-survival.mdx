## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- interpret a survival probability without confusing it with a discount
  factor;
- calculate the default probability of one interval from the survival curve;
- explain what a constant hazard rate means in this toy model;
- calculate the survival probability from a constant hazard rate.

## Survival probability: no default by time t

The [[survival-probability]] $S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t)$ is the probability, under
the [[risk-neutral-probability-measure]] $\mathbb{Q}$, that the reference
entity has not defaulted between [[valuation-time]] $0$ and a future time $t$.
With the local [[credit-default-time]] $\tau$, the survival probability is
defined as:

$$
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t)}
=\explain{risk-neutral-probability-measure}{\mathbb{Q}}
(\explain{credit-default-time}{\tau}>
\explain{payment-time}{t}).
$$

In this lesson, the survival probabilities are inputs: the lesson states their
values and does not calculate them. In practice, a survival curve is fitted to
market prices. The CDS lesson on the credit curve fits one to CDS quotes.

A survival probability is not a discount factor. A survival probability is the
probability of a modeled event. A discount factor converts an amount paid at a
future time to its value at time zero. A survival probability and a discount
factor can have equal values and still mean different things.

We assume that the survival curve starts at one:

$$
S(0,0)=1
$$

We also assume that the survival curve does not increase with time. This
lesson does not estimate survival probabilities from historical data, and it
does not fit them to market quotes. Tuckman and Serrat introduce survival and
default probabilities for CDS valuation[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; Ch. 14 §14.6, printed pp. 367–370, eqs. 14.4–14.7].
Hull distinguishes risk-neutral default probabilities, which are used in
valuation, from real-world default probabilities
[@hull-options-futures; Ch. 23 §23.5, printed pp. 528–530].

## Interval default probability: a decrease in survival

The local [[credit-period-index]] $i$ selects one interval of the survival
schedule, and the [[number-of-credit-periods]] $n$ counts the intervals.
Interval $i$ starts at $t_{i-1}$ and ends at the [[payment-time]]
$\explain{payment-time}{t_i}$, with $t_{i-1}<t_i$. The
[[interval-default-probability]] of interval $i$ is the decrease in survival
probability over the interval:

$$
\explain{interval-default-probability}{\Delta q_i^{\mathbb{Q}}}
=\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t_{i-1})}
-\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t_i)}.
$$

The survival probability at the start of the interval comes first in the
subtraction. Because the survival curve does not increase, this order gives
$\Delta q_i\geq0$. The sum of the interval default probabilities from time zero
to $t_n$ is the cumulative default probability by $t_n$:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta q_i=1-\explain{survival-probability}{S(0,t_n)}.
$$

This identity holds because the sum telescopes: each intermediate survival
probability appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign. The
identity uses two assumptions: $S(0,0)=1$, and at most one modeled default.

## Constant hazard rate: an intensity, not a cumulative probability

In this lesson, the [[hazard-rate]] $\lambda$ is constant. It is a conditional
default intensity, measured per model-year. The hazard rate is not the
probability of default by the end of one year. It is also not a discount rate.
With the local [[credit-small-time-increment]] $\Delta t$, the hazard rate is
defined as the limit of a conditional default probability per unit of time:

$$
\explain{hazard-rate}{\lambda}
=\lim_{\Delta t\downarrow0}
\frac{
\explain{risk-neutral-probability-measure}{\mathbb{Q}}
(t<\explain{credit-default-time}{\tau}\leq t+\Delta t
\mid \explain{credit-default-time}{\tau}>t)
}{\Delta t}.
$$

The probability in this definition is conditional on the event $\tau>t$. So the
hazard rate at time $t$ describes only the paths on which the reference entity
has survived to $t$.

With a constant hazard rate, the survival probability is:

$$
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t)}
=\exp(-\explain{hazard-rate}{\lambda}t).
\label{eq:constant-hazard-survival}
$$

So the cumulative default probability by $t$ is $1-S(0,t)$. This lesson does
not introduce piecewise hazard rates, curve calibration, or a statistical
estimator. Tuckman and Serrat derive the conditional intensity and the
constant-hazard survival probability[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; Ch. 14 §14.6, printed pp. 367–368; Appendix A14.1, printed p. 505, eqs. A14.1–A14.2],
and so does Hull
[@hull-options-futures; Ch. 23 §§23.2 and 23.5, printed pp. 522–523 and 528–530].

  <CompactExample label="One interval drop">

Suppose the one-year survival probability is $S(0,1)=0.97$. With $S(0,0)=1$,
the interval default probability of the first year is:

$$
\explain{interval-default-probability}{\Delta q_1}=1-0.97=0.03=3\%.
$$

The 3% is the probability of default during the first year. It is not
discounted.

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Three years at constant hazard">

Let $\lambda=0.02$ per model-year and $t=3$ model-years. Substituting these
values into [[eq-constant-hazard-survival]] gives the three-year survival
probability:

$$
S(0,3)=\exp(-0.02\times3)=\exp(-0.06)\approx0.941765.
$$

The cumulative default probability by three years is:

$$
1-S(0,3)\approx0.058235=5.8235\%.
$$

The cumulative default probability is not $\lambda t=6\%$. The product
$\lambda t$ is the exponent in the survival probability. It is not the exact
cumulative default probability.

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Two unequal interval drops">

Consider this survival curve:

| Interval index $i$ | Time $\explain{payment-time}{t_i}$, model-years | Survival $\explain{survival-probability}{S(0,t_i)}$ | Interval default $\Delta q_i$ |
| -----------------: | ----------------------------------------------: | --------------------------------------------------: | ----------------------------: |
|                  0 |                                               0 |                                                1.00 |                             — |
|                  1 |                                               1 |                                                0.97 |                          0.03 |
|                  2 |                                               2 |                                                0.92 |                          0.05 |

The interval default probability of the second interval, 0.05, is larger than
that of the first interval, 0.03, although both intervals are one model-year
long. The cumulative two-year default probability is $0.03+0.05=0.08=1-0.92$.

## Model boundary and review note

This lesson uses a toy model with at most one default, a constant hazard
rate, exact model-year times, and survival probabilities that are inputs. The
model omits:

- calendar dates, day counts, and business-day adjustments;
- stochastic interest rates;
- dependence between interest rates and default;
- estimation and calibration.

The lesson makes no claim that pricing-model probabilities are forecasts of
realized default frequencies.
