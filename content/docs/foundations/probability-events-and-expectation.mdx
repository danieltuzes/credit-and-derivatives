## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify events that form a disjoint and exhaustive partition;
- calculate a conditional probability;
- calculate an expectation when a random value is constant on each event;
- use conditional event means when the value varies inside an event.

## Outcomes, events, and probabilities

An **outcome** is one possible result of a modeled experiment. An **event** is
a specified set of outcomes. Use the local [[probability-event]] $A$ for
one such set and the local [[probability-measure]] $\mathbb{P}$ for the
rule that assigns probabilities to events. In this lesson, $\mathbb{P}$ is a general
probability measure. A later finance lesson uses $\mathbb{P}$ only for
real-world probabilities and introduces a different symbol for pricing
probabilities. This lesson does not choose between the two.

In this lesson, a finite partition is a finite list of events that are:

- **disjoint:** no outcome belongs to two different partition events; and
- **exhaustive:** every possible outcome belongs to one of them.

Use the local [[probability-event-index]] $j$ to select an event and the
local [[probability-partition-size]] $m$ to count the events. For a
disjoint and exhaustive list $A_1,\ldots,A_m$, additivity of the probability
measure gives the sum of the event probabilities:

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(A_j)=1.
$$

If the events overlap, the sum of their probabilities can count some outcomes
twice. If the events are not exhaustive, the sum omits some outcomes. These
event and additivity definitions follow Shreve's probability-space
treatment[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 1 §1.1, printed pp. 1-3, Def. 1.1.2 and eqs. 1.1.2-1.1.5].

## Conditional probability restricts the outcomes

Suppose the question asks for the probability of event $A$ **given** another
event. Write the conditioning event as $\explain{probability-event}{B}$. When
$\mathbb{P}(\explain{probability-event}{B})>0$, the conditional probability
of $A$ given $\explain{probability-event}{B}$ is defined as:

$$
\mathbb{P}(A\mid\explain{probability-event}{B})
=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap\explain{probability-event}{B})}
{\mathbb{P}(\explain{probability-event}{B})}.
$$

The denominator is the probability of the outcomes that the condition “given
$\explain{probability-event}{B}$” keeps. For example, suppose 30% of all
outcomes are in $\explain{probability-event}{B}$, and 12% are in both $A$ and
$\explain{probability-event}{B}$. Then the conditional probability is:

$$
\mathbb{P}(A\mid\explain{probability-event}{B})
=\frac{0.12}{0.30}=0.40.
$$

In a default model, consider the probability of “default during this period
given survival to its start.” Its denominator is the probability of survival to
the start of the period. This conditional probability is different from the
unconditional probability of default by a horizon. Shreve develops conditioning as averaging within the information
or event retained by the condition[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 2 §2.3, printed pp. 66-69, eqs. 2.3.4-2.3.17 and Def. 2.3.1].

## Expectation from disjoint events

Use the local [[probability-random-value]] $X$ for a numerical value
determined by the outcome and the [[expectation]] operator $\mathbb{E}$ for
its probability-weighted average. In this lesson every random value is constant
on each event of a finite partition, so the general integral definition of
$\mathbb{E}$ reduces to the weighted sum below. The expectation has the same
unit as $X$; a random USD payment has an expected value in USD, not in
percent.

This section gives two formulas. The first formula requires $X$ to be
constant on each event. The second formula does not.

### Exact value on every event

First suppose $X$ is constant on every partition event. Use the local
[[probability-event-value]] $x_j$ for its value on $A_j$. Then the
expectation is the sum of the event values, each multiplied by the probability
of its event:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]
=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_j\mathbb{P}(A_j),
\qquad X=x_j\text{ on }A_j.
\label{eq:expectation-event-values}
$$

Shreve states the expectation of a finite-valued random variable in the form
of [[eq-expectation-event-values]][@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 1 §1.3, printed pp. 13-18, Thms. 1.3.1 and 1.3.4].

### Value varies inside an event

If $X$ is not constant inside $A_j$, one representative value is in general not
exact. For each event with positive probability, replace the value with the
conditional mean of $X$ inside that event. The expectation is then:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]
=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(A_j)\mathbb{E}[X\mid A_j].
\label{eq:expectation-conditional-events}
$$

An event with zero probability contributes zero to the sum and can be omitted.
Its conditional mean need not be defined.

The partition identity [[eq-expectation-conditional-events]] follows by splitting an expectation across disjoint events and
using the partial-averaging property of conditional expectation. Shreve gives
the disjoint-event integral split and the unconditional-average property of
conditional expectation[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 1 §1.3, printed p. 17, Remark 1.3.2; Ch. 2 §2.3, printed pp. 68-72, eqs. 2.3.16-2.3.17 and 2.3.25].

<CompactExamples summary="Worked examples (3)">
  <CompactExample label="Constant value on each event">

Suppose $X$ is a payment in USD. It equals USD 0, USD 50, or USD
100 on three disjoint exhaustive events with probabilities 0.10, 0.20, and
0.70.

| Event | Constant payment, USD | Probability | Contribution, USD |
| ----: | --------------------: | ----------: | ----------------: |
| $A_1$ |                     0 |        0.10 |              0.00 |
| $A_2$ |                    50 |        0.20 |             10.00 |
| $A_3$ |                   100 |        0.70 |             70.00 |

The expectation is the sum of the contributions:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]
=0\times0.10+50\times0.20+100\times0.70
=\text{USD }80.
$$

The expectation of USD 80 is a probability-weighted average. An expectation
need not be one of the possible payments, and in this example it is not.

  </CompactExample>
  <CompactExample label="Conditional means inside events">

Suppose three disjoint exhaustive events have probabilities 0.01, 0.02, and
0.97, and the payment varies inside each event. The conditional means of the
payment are USD 40, USD 30, and USD 0. The exact expectation from these
conditional means is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]
=0.01\times40+0.02\times30+0.97\times0
=\text{USD }1.00.
$$

USD 40 is the average payment inside the first event. The example does not
claim that every outcome in the first event pays USD 40.

  </CompactExample>
  <CompactExample label="Default inside a period">

For a CDS period, one event can be “default occurs in this period.” A
protection payment discounted at the actual default time varies within that
event. Accrued premium also varies within the event, because a default near
the end of the period accrues more premium than a default near its start.

So one midpoint value for the whole period is an approximation. The exact
contribution of the event is the interval default probability multiplied by the
conditional mean of the discounted payment inside the period. If the period is
divided into smaller disjoint default-time intervals, the sum has more terms.
In a continuous-time model, the limit of this sum is an integral over the
default time. The later CDS lesson gives the hazard-rate probabilities and the
cash-flow formulas for that integral.

  </CompactExample>
</CompactExamples>

## Model boundary and review note

This lesson teaches finite partitions and the idea of the limit of a
refinement. It does
not construct probability spaces, prove measure-theoretic integration, estimate
probabilities from data, calibrate risk-neutral probabilities, or evaluate the
default-time integrals used by a CDS model. A later lesson distinguishes
real-world and risk-neutral measures.

The lesson paraphrases the cited textbook; it remains `draft` pending human
verification of the printed locators, formulas, examples, and notation
bindings.
