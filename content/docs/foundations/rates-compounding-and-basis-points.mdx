## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- interpret a nominal annual quote together with its frequency;
- calculate the rate applied in one compounding period;
- convert between decimal rates, percentages, and basis points.

## A rate quote needs its compounding convention

The phrase “an annual rate of 6%” does not determine the compounding unless
its convention is stated. In this lesson, write the [[nominal-annual-rate]] as
$j^{(m)}$ and pair it with the [[compounding-frequency]] $m$. Write the
resulting [[periodic-rate]] as $r_m$.

In this lesson, $t$ denotes a [[payment-time]] without a row index. The
[[valuation-time]] is $0$. Use the local [[number-of-compounding-periods]]
$N$ for the number of times the periodic rate is applied, and write the resulting [[accumulation-factor]] as $A(0,t)$.

Under the nominal-rate convention of this lesson, the periodic rate is the
nominal annual rate divided by the compounding frequency:

$$
r_m=\frac{j^{(m)}}{m}.
$$

This division is a convention of the quote. It is not a conversion rule for
every kind of annual rate.

<CompactExamples summary="Worked examples (3)">
  <CompactExample label="Nominal 6%, compounded semiannually">

With $j^{(2)}=0.06$ and $m=2$, the periodic rate is:

$$
r_2=\frac{0.06}{2}=0.03.
$$

The model applies the periodic rate of 3% once every half-year. Over
$t=1.5$ years, the local [[number-of-compounding-periods]] is $N=mt=3$. So
the [[accumulation-factor]] is:

$$
A(0,1.5)
=(1+0.03)^3
=1.092727.
$$

One unit at [[valuation-time]] accumulates to 1.092727 units at the
[[payment-time]] 1.5 years later.

  </CompactExample>
  <CompactExample label="Nominal is not effective annual">

Under the same convention, the effective annual rate, i.e., the growth over
one year of compounding, is:

$$
(1.03)^2-1=0.0609=6.09\%.
$$

So the nominal annual rate of 6% and the effective annual rate of 6.09% are
different quantities. The name “annual rate” without a convention does not
distinguish them.

  </CompactExample>
  <CompactExample label="Basis-point arithmetic">

A [[basis-point]] (bp) is a unit for rate differences. One basis point is
0.0001, i.e., 0.01%:

$$
25\,\mathrm{bp}
=25\times0.0001
=0.0025
=0.25\%.
$$

A change from 4.10% to 4.35% is $0.25$ percentage points (25 bp). Write the
unit explicitly, because the number 25 without a unit is ambiguous.

  </CompactExample>
</CompactExamples>

## Common unit boundary

| Representation                | Same rate |
| ----------------------------- | --------: |
| Decimal used by domain code   |    0.0435 |
| Percentage shown to a learner |     4.35% |
| Basis points above zero       |    435 bp |

A lab converts a percentage input to a decimal. The domain functions take
decimal rates.

## Model boundary and review note

This lesson does not introduce continuous compounding, a general conversion to
effective rates, day counts, or a term structure.

The nominal-quote-plus-frequency convention and the basis-point unit follow
Tuckman & Serrat[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; §2.1, rates quoted as annual rates over a term of fixed-length periods, simple or compound; §2.4, eq. 2.17, the periodic-compounding form; a basis point is 0.01%, Ch. 0 footnote and Ch. 3]. The lesson stays `draft` pending human confirmation of the printed
locators.
