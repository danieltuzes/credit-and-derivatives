## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- distinguish a real-world probability from a risk-neutral pricing weight;
- explain why changing measure does not remove uncertainty or volatility;
- calculate an expected discounted payoff in a finite-state model;
- state why a simple CDS model can omit a separate volatility input without
  claiming that volatility never matters.

## Two measures for two different uses

Valuation is at [[valuation-time]] $0$. Let the local
[[risk-neutral-pricing-horizon]] $T$ be a future time in exact model-years,
and let the [[terminal-random-payoff]] $X_T$ be the signed USD amount that a
claim holder receives or pays at $T$.

A probability measure assigns mutually consistent weights to events. The
[[real-world-probability-measure]] $\mathbb{P}$ describes the modeled
probabilities of events in the real world. It is the measure for a forecast.
The [[risk-neutral-probability-measure]] $\mathbb{Q}$ gives the weights of
an arbitrage-free pricing model. So the same event can have different
probabilities under $\mathbb{P}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$. Shreve makes this
distinction between the actual and the risk-neutral measure in the
introduction of the change of measure
[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 1 §1.6, printed pp. 32-35].
Hull makes the same distinction between forecasting and valuation
[@hull-options-futures; Ch. 12 §12.2, printed pp. 257-260; Ch. 14 §14.7, printed pp. 311-313].

> **There is no “risk-free measure” in this model.** The measure is
> **risk-neutral**. “Risk-free” describes the cash account and discounting
> convention used as the pricing benchmark. It does not describe the event
> probabilities, and it does not make the payoff safe.

The name “risk-neutral” also does not assume that every investor is
indifferent to risk. Risk preferences and risk premia are among the reasons why
$\mathbb{P}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ differ. The pricing calculation uses
$\mathbb{Q}$ after $\mathbb{Q}$ has been selected or calibrated.

## From a partition to a price

In a no-arbitrage model with the cash account as numeraire, $\mathbb{Q}$ is
chosen so that discounted traded prices are martingales. In words:
after discounting with the cash account, today's traded price equals the
conditional $\mathbb{Q}$-expectation of its discounted future value. Shreve states the martingale
condition and its discounted-payoff consequence
[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 5 §5.2.2, printed pp. 214-217, eqs. 5.2.22-5.2.24; §5.2.4, printed pp. 218-219, eqs. 5.2.29-5.2.31]. Tuckman and Serrat give the same expected-discounted-value
construction in a finite-state fixed-income setting
[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; Ch. 7 §7.3, printed pp. 182-184, eqs. 7.7-7.8].

Write the [[expectation]] under the [[risk-neutral-probability-measure]] as
$\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}$. It is the same averaging operator, taken with the
pricing weights of $\mathbb{Q}$. Use the local
[[risk-neutral-scenario-index]] $j$ and [[risk-neutral-scenario-count]]
$m$. Let $x_j$ be the local
[[risk-neutral-scenario-payoff]], in USD at $T$, and let $q_j$ be its
dimensionless [[risk-neutral-scenario-probability]]. For disjoint,
exhaustive scenarios on which the payoff is constant, the risk-neutral
expected payoff is:

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_T]
=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_jq_j,
\qquad
q_j\ge 0,
\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^{m}q_j=1.
\label{eq:risk-neutral-expected-payoff}
$$

This expected payoff is in USD at $T$. It is not a present value. Let
the input [[discount-factor]] be $D(0,T)$, in USD at time zero per USD at
$T$. The claim's [[present-value]] is the discounted risk-neutral expected
payoff:

$$
PV_0
=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[D(0,T)X_T\right]
=D(0,T)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_T]
=D(0,T)\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_jq_j.
\label{eq:risk-neutral-present-value}
$$

The second equality uses this lesson's assumption that $D(0,T)$ is
deterministic. If the discount factor is random, it must in general stay inside the
expectation. The expectation of a product equals the product of the
expectations only under additional assumptions.

## Worked example

Suppose $D(0,T)=0.95$ and the claim has two disjoint, exhaustive outcomes:

| Scenario $j$ | Risk-neutral probability $q_j$ | Payoff $x_j$, USD at $T$ | Contribution $q_jx_j$, USD at $T$ |
| -----------: | -----------------------------: | -----------------------: | --------------------------------: |
|            1 |                           0.25 |                      120 |                             30.00 |
|            2 |                           0.75 |                       40 |                             30.00 |

The risk-neutral expected payoff [[eq-risk-neutral-expected-payoff]] and
time-zero value [[eq-risk-neutral-present-value]] are

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_T]
=0.25(120)+0.75(40)
=\text{USD }60\text{ at }T,
$$

$$
PV_0
=0.95(60)
=\text{USD }57\text{ at time }0.
$$

No $\mathbb{P}$ probabilities enter this price, because the example states
the $\mathbb{Q}$ pricing weights as inputs. An estimate of the real-world
expected payoff would be a different calculation.

## Uncertainty remains

A change from $\mathbb{P}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$ changes the weights of the modeled
outcomes. It does not remove the randomness. In the standard change-of-measure
result for a diffusion, the drift changes and the diffusion coefficient stays
the same
[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 5 §5.2.2, printed pp. 214-217, especially eqs. 5.2.22-5.2.24]. So volatility remains a model
input for nonlinear claims such as options. A restricted linear model can omit
a separate volatility input for reasons specific to its product. The lesson on
that product states those reasons.

## Advanced boundary: uniqueness

The pricing measure need not be unique. Under the usual technical conditions,
the absence of arbitrage is tied to the existence of a suitable martingale
measure. Uniqueness of the measure requires a stronger condition: the modeled
market must be complete. This section is a boundary note, and the assessment
of this lesson does not test it. Shreve develops the existence and uniqueness distinction
[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 5 §§5.4.3-5.4.4, printed pp. 228-233].

## Model boundary and review note

This lesson uses one terminal payoff, a finite scenario partition, deterministic
risk-free discounting, and input $\mathbb{Q}$ weights. It does not estimate
$\mathbb{P}$, calibrate $\mathbb{Q}$, generate a yield curve, treat a stochastic
numeraire, establish market completeness, or value counterparty, liquidity,
funding, or option effects.
