## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- distinguish a real-world probability from a risk-neutral pricing weight;
- explain why changing measure does not remove uncertainty or volatility;
- calculate an expected discounted payoff in a finite-state model;
- state why a simple CDS model can omit a separate volatility input without
  claiming that volatility never matters.

## Two measures answer two different questions

Stand at [[valuation-time]] $0$. Let the local
[[risk-neutral-pricing-horizon]] $T$ be a future time in exact model-years,
and let the [[terminal-random-payoff]] $X_T$ be the signed USD amount a
claim holder receives or pays then.

A probability measure assigns mutually consistent weights to events. The
[[real-world-probability-measure]] $\mathbb{P}$ is intended to describe
modeled actual-world likelihoods: it is the relevant measure for a forecast.
The [[risk-neutral-probability-measure]] $\mathbb{Q}$ instead supplies
weights for an arbitrage-consistent pricing model. The same event can therefore
have different probabilities under $\mathbb{P}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$. Shreve makes
this actual-versus-risk-neutral distinction when introducing change of measure
[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 1 §1.6, printed pp. 32-35];
Hull makes the same forecasting-versus-valuation distinction
[@hull-options-futures; Ch. 12 §12.2, printed pp. 257-260; Ch. 14 §14.7, printed pp. 311-313].

> **There is no “risk-free measure” in this model.** The measure is
> **risk-neutral**. “Risk-free” describes the cash account and discounting
> convention used as the pricing benchmark. It does not describe the event
> probabilities, and it does not make the payoff safe.

The name also does not assume that every investor is indifferent to risk.
Risk preferences and risk premia help make $\mathbb{P}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$
different; the pricing calculation uses $\mathbb{Q}$ after it has been selected
or calibrated.

## From a partition to a price

In a no-arbitrage model with the cash account as numeraire, $\mathbb{Q}$ is
chosen so that discounted traded prices are martingales. In plain language,
after applying the benchmark discounting, today's traded price equals its
conditional $\mathbb{Q}$-weighted future value. Shreve states the martingale
condition and its discounted-payoff consequence
[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 5 §5.2.2, printed pp. 214-217, eqs. 5.2.22-5.2.24; §5.2.4, printed pp. 218-219, eqs. 5.2.29-5.2.31]. Tuckman and Serrat give the same expected-discounted-value
construction in a finite-state fixed-income setting
[@tuckman-serrat-fixed-income; Ch. 7 §7.3, printed pp. 182-184, eqs. 7.7-7.8].

To calculate the [[risk-neutral-expectation]], use the local
[[risk-neutral-scenario-index]] $j$ and
[[risk-neutral-scenario-count]] $m$. Let $x_j$ be the local
[[risk-neutral-scenario-payoff]], in USD at $T$, and let $q_j$ be its
dimensionless [[risk-neutral-scenario-probability]]. For disjoint,
exhaustive scenarios on which the payoff is constant,

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_T]
=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_jq_j,
\qquad
q_j\ge 0,
\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^{m}q_j=1.
$$

This expected payoff is measured in future USD at $T$. It is not yet a
time-zero value. Let the supplied [[discount-factor]] be $D(0,T)$, in
time-zero USD per USD at $T$. The claim's [[present-value]] is

$$
PV_0
=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[D(0,T)X_T\right]
=D(0,T)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_T]
=D(0,T)\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_jq_j.
$$

The second equality uses this lesson's assumption that $D(0,T)$ is
deterministic. If discounting is random, it must generally remain inside the
expectation; replacing an expectation of a product by a product of expectations
would require additional assumptions.

## Worked example

Suppose $D(0,T)=0.95$ and the claim has two disjoint, exhaustive outcomes:

| Scenario $j$ | Risk-neutral probability $q_j$ | Payoff $x_j$, USD at $T$ | Contribution $q_jx_j$, USD at $T$ |
| -----------: | -----------------------------: | -----------------------: | --------------------------------: |
|            1 |                           0.25 |                      120 |                             30.00 |
|            2 |                           0.75 |                       40 |                             30.00 |

The risk-neutral expected payoff and time-zero value are

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_T]
=0.25(120)+0.75(40)
=\text{USD }60\text{ at }T,
$$

$$
PV_0
=0.95(60)
=\text{USD }57\text{ at time }0.
$$

No $\mathbb{P}$ probabilities enter this price because the example explicitly
supplies $\mathbb{Q}$ pricing weights. Estimating a real-world expected payoff
would be a different calculation.

## Uncertainty remains

Changing from $\mathbb{P}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$ reweights modeled outcomes; it does
not erase randomness. In the standard diffusion change-of-measure result, the
drift changes while the diffusion coefficient remains
[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 5 §5.2.2, printed pp. 214-217, especially eqs. 5.2.22-5.2.24]. Volatility therefore remains a model
input for nonlinear claims such as options. Product-specific reasons why a
restricted linear model may omit a separate volatility input belong beside that
product model rather than in this foundation.

## Advanced boundary: uniqueness

The supplied pricing measure need not be unique. Under the usual technical
conditions, absence of arbitrage is tied to existence of a suitable martingale
measure, while uniqueness requires the stronger condition that the modeled
market be complete. This is a boundary note rather than a competency assessed
on this page. Shreve develops the existence and uniqueness distinction
[@shreve-stochastic-calculus-finance-ii; Ch. 5 §§5.4.3-5.4.4, printed pp. 228-233].

## Model boundary and review note

This lesson uses one terminal payoff, a finite scenario partition, deterministic
risk-free discounting, and supplied $\mathbb{Q}$ weights. It does not estimate
$\mathbb{P}$, calibrate $\mathbb{Q}$, generate a yield curve, treat a stochastic
numeraire, establish market completeness, or value counterparty, liquidity,
funding, or option effects.

All source records, notation, formulas, examples, and answer keys remain
`draft` pending independent human source, quantitative, and editorial review.
