## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- explain why identical dated state-contingent cash flows have the same value under the stated assumptions;
- distinguish exact replication from matching only an expected cash flow;
- calculate a claim value from signed positions in a replicating portfolio.

## Compare cash flows, not labels

The law of one price applies when two packages produce the same
\term\{signed-cash-flow\} at every relevant future date and in every modeled
state. Their legal names can differ. What matters is the full cash-flow vector
from the same perspective.

If the packages had different values at \term\{valuation-time\} $0$, the
lesson's frictionless model would permit buying the cheaper package and selling
the dearer one. The future cash flows cancel, leaving the initial difference.
That is the no-arbitrage link behind the pricing rule. Tuckman and Serrat state
the law of one price and connect deviations from it to an arbitrage trade built
from a replicating portfolio\cite\{tuckman-serrat-fixed-income\}\{Ch. 1 §§1.3-1.4,
printed pp. 53-57, law of one price and arbitrage replication\}.

Matching only a mean is not enough. A payment of USD 100 only in an up state is
not the same cash flow as USD 100 only in a down state, even if someone assigns
the two states equal probability.

## Value the replicating portfolio

Use the local \term\{replication-security-index\} $i$ to select one security
and the local \term\{replication-security-count\} $n$ to count them. Let the
signed \term\{replication-security-quantity\} be $a_i$ and the current
\term\{replication-security-value\} be $V_{0,i}$. When that portfolio exactly
matches the claim's future cash flows, the \term\{replicated-claim-value\} is

$$
V_0^{\mathrm{claim}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iV_{0,i}.
$$

A positive $a_i$ is long; a negative $a_i$ is short. The formula uses current
values because the future matching has already done the economic work.

Suppose a claim is replicated by two units of security A worth USD 12 each and
one unit of security B worth USD 7. The replicating portfolio, and therefore the
claim, has value

$$
V_0^{\mathrm{claim}}=2(12)+1(7)=\text{USD }31.
$$

## Where this bridge leads

Forward prices use cash-and-carry replication. Put-call parity compares two
portfolios with identical expiry cash flows. A binomial option model constructs
a state-by-state replicating portfolio. Risk-neutral valuation is another way
to calculate that same no-arbitrage value inside a specified model.

## Model boundary and review note

Real trades can have bid-ask spreads, different borrowing and lending rates,
short-sale constraints, collateral, taxes, liquidity differences, and execution
risk. Those effects can prevent a textbook arbitrage from being executable.
This lesson establishes a deliberately frictionless pricing benchmark rather
than claiming that every observed price difference is free profit.
