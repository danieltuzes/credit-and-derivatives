## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- interpret a nominal annual quote together with its frequency;
- calculate the rate applied in one compounding period;
- convert between decimal rates, percentages, and basis points.

## One label is not enough

The phrase “an annual rate of 6%” is incomplete for compounding unless its
convention is stated. In this slice, write the \term\{nominal-annual-rate\} as
$j^{(m)}$ and pair it with the \term\{compounding-frequency\} $m$. Write the
resulting \term\{periodic-rate\} as $r_m$.

On this page, $t$ is the shorter generic-horizon form of the shared
\term\{payment-time\}. The \term\{valuation-time\} is $0$. Use the local
\term\{number-of-compounding-periods\} $N$ for the number of rate applications,
and write the resulting \term\{accumulation-factor\} as $A(0,t)$.

Under the stated nominal-rate convention, the periodic rate is

$$
r_m=\frac{j^{(m)}}{m}.
$$

This is a declared convention, not a universal conversion rule for every kind
of annual rate.

<CompactExamples summary="Worked examples (3)">
  <CompactExample label="Nominal 6%, compounded semiannually">

With $j^{(2)}=0.06$ and $m=2$,

$$
r_2=\frac{0.06}{2}=0.03.
$$

The model applies 3% once every half-year. Over $t=1.5$ years, the local
\term\{number-of-compounding-periods\} is $N=mt=3$, so the
\term\{accumulation-factor\} is

$$
A(0,1.5)
=(1+0.03)^3
=1.092727.
$$

One unit at \term\{valuation-time\} grows to 1.092727 model units at the
\term\{payment-time\} 1.5 years later.

  </CompactExample>
  <CompactExample label="Nominal is not effective annual">

Under the same nominal 6% semiannual convention, one year of compounding gives

$$
(1.03)^2-1=0.0609=6.09\%.
$$

Thus the 6% input and 6.09% one-year effective growth rate are different
quantities. The semantic key prevents an author from calling both merely
“annualRate.”

  </CompactExample>
  <CompactExample label="Basis-point arithmetic">

A \term\{basis-point\} is a unit for rate differences:

$$
25\,\mathrm{bp}
=25\times0.0001
=0.0025
=0.25\%.
$$

A move from 4.10% to 4.35% is therefore $0.25$ percentage points, or 25 bp.
Write the unit explicitly; the bare number 25 is ambiguous.

  </CompactExample>
</CompactExamples>

## Common unit boundary

| Representation                | Same rate |
| ----------------------------- | --------: |
| Decimal used by domain code   |    0.0435 |
| Percentage shown to a learner |     4.35% |
| Basis points above zero       |    435 bp |

User interfaces convert percentages at their boundary. Domain functions take
decimal rates.

## Model boundary and review note

This page does not introduce continuous compounding, effective-rate conversion
as a general API, day counts, or a term structure.

The nominal-quote-plus-frequency convention and the basis-point unit follow
Tuckman & Serrat\cite\{tuckman-serrat-fixed-income\}\{§2.1, rates quoted as
annual rates over a term of fixed-length periods, simple or compound; §2.4, eq.
2.17, the periodic-compounding form; a basis point is 0.01%, Ch. 0 footnote and
Ch. 3\}. The lesson stays `draft` pending human confirmation of the printed
locators.
