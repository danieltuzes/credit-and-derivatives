## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- read an ordered schedule of payment amounts and times;
- state the unit and clock used by a timeline;
- apply a consistent sign convention from a named perspective.

## Start with events, not formulas

A financial calculation begins with a list of events. The
\term\{valuation-time\}, written $0$, is the common origin for the page's
clock. Use the local \term\{payment-index\} $k$ to identify one row; it is not
itself a time or date. At row $k$, write the \term\{signed-cash-flow\} as
$CF_k$ and its \term\{payment-time\} as $t_k$.

$$
CF_k \quad\text{occurs at}\quad t_k.
$$

The shared subscript connects each amount to its matching time while preserving
the distinct meanings of amount, time, and row index.

## Example 1: read a timeline

Suppose, in a simplified primary-issuance example, a bondholder pays an issuer
USD 980 now, receives USD 30 after one year, and receives USD 1,030 after two
years.

| Payment index $k$ | Payment time $t_k$, years from time 0 | Signed cash flow $CF_k$, USD | Event                         |
| ----------------: | ------------------------------------: | ---------------------------: | ----------------------------- |
|                 0 |                                     0 |                         -980 | Purchase price paid           |
|                 1 |                                     1 |                          +30 | Coupon received               |
|                 2 |                                     2 |                       +1,030 | Coupon and principal received |

This table does **not** say whether USD 980 is a fair price. It only describes
who exchanges what, and when.

## Example 2: reverse the perspective

From that issuer's perspective, every amount above changes sign:

|    Time | Bondholder |     Issuer |
| ------: | ---------: | ---------: |
| 0 years |   USD -980 |   USD +980 |
|  1 year |    USD +30 |    USD -30 |
| 2 years | USD +1,030 | USD -1,030 |

Changing perspective does not move the dates or change absolute amounts.
Adding cash flows from opposite perspectives without first reconciling their
signs would mix two different models.

## Example 3: same amount, different time

USD +100 at $t_1=0.5$ years and USD +100 at $t_2=2$ years are two distinct
events. The amounts match; the times do not. A later lesson introduces the
discount factors needed to compare them at time zero.
