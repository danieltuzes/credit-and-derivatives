## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- interpret a supplied survival probability without confusing it with a
  discount factor;
- calculate the probability assigned to default inside one interval;
- explain what a constant hazard rate means in this toy model;
- calculate survival from a supplied constant hazard rate.

## Survival answers a dated probability question

The \term\{survival-probability\} $S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t)$ is the supplied
probability, under the \term\{risk-neutral-probability-measure\}
$\mathbb{Q}$, that the reference entity has not defaulted between
\term\{valuation-time\} $0$ and future time $t$. With the local
\term\{credit-default-time\} $\tau$,

$$
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t)}
=\explain{risk-neutral-probability-measure}{\mathbb{Q}}
(\explain{credit-default-time}{\tau}>
\explain{payment-time}{t}).
$$

This meaning is deliberately separate from time value. A survival probability
describes a modeled event; a discount factor converts a future unit to value at
time zero. Equal numbers would not make those concepts interchangeable.

The toy curve begins at

$$
S(0,0)=1
$$

and does not increase with time. The page does not estimate these probabilities
from data or market quotes. Tuckman and Serrat introduce survival and default
probabilities for CDS valuation\cite\{tuckman-serrat-fixed-income\}\{Ch. 14
§14.6, printed pp. 367–370, eqs. 14.4–14.7\}; Hull distinguishes risk-neutral
default probabilities used in valuation from real-world probabilities
\cite\{hull-options-futures\}\{Ch. 23 §23.5, printed pp. 528–530\}.

## An interval probability is a drop in survival

Use the local \term\{credit-period-index\} $i$ to select one of the
\term\{number-of-credit-periods\} $n$. The matching
\term\{payment-time\} is written explicitly as
$\explain{payment-time}{t_i}$. For ordered times, the
\term\{interval-default-probability\} is

$$
\explain{interval-default-probability}{\Delta q_i^{\mathbb{Q}}}
=\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t_{i-1})}
-\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t_i)}.
$$

The subtraction order matters. A non-increasing survival curve gives
$\Delta q_i\geq0$. Adding interval probabilities from time zero through a
horizon gives the cumulative probability assigned to default by that horizon:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta q_i=1-\explain{survival-probability}{S(0,t_n)}.
$$

That telescoping identity uses the assumptions $S(0,0)=1$ and at most one
modeled default.

## Constant hazard is an intensity, not a cumulative probability

The \term\{hazard-rate\} $\lambda$ is a constant conditional default intensity
per model-year in this deliberately narrow model. It is not the probability of
default by the end of one year and is not a discount rate. Using the local
\term\{credit-small-time-increment\} $\Delta t$, its conditional definition is

$$
\explain{hazard-rate}{\lambda}
=\lim_{\Delta t\downarrow0}
\frac{
\explain{risk-neutral-probability-measure}{\mathbb{Q}}
(t<\explain{credit-default-time}{\tau}\leq t+\Delta t
\mid \explain{credit-default-time}{\tau}>t)
}{\Delta t}.
$$

The conditioning matters: the population at risk at time $t$ contains only
paths that have survived to $t$.

With constant hazard, the model assigns

$$
\explain{survival-probability}{S^{\mathbb{Q}}(0,t)}
=\exp(-\explain{hazard-rate}{\lambda}t).
$$

The matching cumulative default probability by $t$ is therefore
$1-S(0,t)$. This lesson does not introduce piecewise hazard rates, curve
calibration, or a statistical estimator. The conditional intensity and
constant-hazard exponential survival relation are developed in Tuckman and
Serrat\cite\{tuckman-serrat-fixed-income\}\{Ch. 14 §14.6, printed pp. 367–368;
Appendix A14.1, printed p. 505, eqs. A14.1–A14.2\} and Hull
\cite\{hull-options-futures\}\{Ch. 23 §§23.2 and 23.5, printed pp. 522–523 and
528–530\}.

  <CompactExample label="One interval drop">

Suppose the supplied one-year survival probability is $S(0,1)=0.97$. Starting
from $S(0,0)=1$, the probability assigned to default during the first year is

$$
\explain{interval-default-probability}{\Delta q_1}=1-0.97=0.03=3\%.
$$

The 3% is an interval event probability. No discounting has been performed.

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Three years at constant hazard">

Let $\lambda=0.02$ per model-year and $t=3$ model-years. Then

$$
S(0,3)=\exp(-0.02\times3)=\exp(-0.06)\approx0.941765.
$$

The cumulative probability assigned to default by three years is

$$
1-S(0,3)\approx0.058235=5.8235\%.
$$

It is not $\lambda t=6\%$; that product is the exponent in this model, not the
exact cumulative default probability.

  </CompactExample>

  <CompactExample label="Two unequal interval drops">

Consider this supplied curve:

| Interval index $i$ | Time $\explain{payment-time}{t_i}$, model-years | Survival $\explain{survival-probability}{S(0,t_i)}$ | Interval default $\Delta q_i$ |
| -----------------: | ----------------------------------------------: | --------------------------------------------------: | ----------------------------: |
|                  0 |                                               0 |                                                1.00 |                             — |
|                  1 |                                               1 |                                                0.97 |                          0.03 |
|                  2 |                                               2 |                                                0.92 |                          0.05 |

The second interval receives more default probability than the first, even
though both intervals are one model-year long. The cumulative two-year default
probability is $0.03+0.05=0.08=1-0.92$.

## Model boundary and review note

This is a one-default, constant-hazard teaching model with exact model-year
times and supplied pricing probabilities. It omits calendar dates, day counts,
business-day adjustments, stochastic rates, dependence between rates and
default, estimation, and calibration. It makes no claim that pricing-model
probabilities are forecasts of realized default frequencies.

All materially changed source locators, formulas, examples, notation, and
answer keys remain \`draft\` pending independent human review.
