## What you will be able to do

After this lesson, you should be able to read a deterministic discount curve,
state its interpolation boundary, and calculate a discount factor between two
future dates.

## A curve is a dated collection, not one yield

At \term\{valuation-time\}, the earlier \term\{discount-factor\} $D(0,t)$ valued a
deterministic payment at one \term\{payment-time\}. A discount curve supplies
that value across many times. Every node must keep the same currency, valuation date, and
collateral/funding basis. Mixing bases does not create one coherent curve.

This slice accepts positive nodes and sets $D(0,0)=1$. It interpolates the
logarithm of the factor linearly between nodes and refuses to extrapolate. That
is an explicit computational convention, not a claim that log-linear
interpolation is uniquely correct.

A factor may exceed one. For example, $D(0,1)=1.01$ implies a negative
continuously compounded one-year zero rate. Positivity is required; being
below one is not.

## Discount between future times

Let local \term\{forward-discount-start-time\} $t$ precede local
\term\{forward-discount-end-time\} $T$. The \term\{forward-discount-factor\} is

$$
\explain{forward-discount-factor}{Z(t,T)}
=\frac{\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,T)}}
{\explain{discount-factor}{D(0,t)}}.
$$

The units are time-$t$ currency per one unit of time-$T$ currency. If
$D(0,1)=0.98$ and $D(0,3)=0.94$, then

$$
Z(1,3)=\frac{0.94}{0.98}=0.9591836735.
$$

The glyph $Z$ is deliberate: shared $D$ remains bound to a factor from
valuation time, so the new meaning does not silently rebind it.

This ratio is essential for multi-period valuation: each lattice node needs
the factor over its next period, not an indiscriminate reuse of a time-zero
factor.

## Scope boundary

The curve is supplied, deterministic, and uncalibrated. Later state-dependent
lessons supply one-period factors by node; they do not assume that today's
future-to-future factor will actually be observed in every future state.
